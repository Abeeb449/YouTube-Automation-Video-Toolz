# Why Do Sea Lions Fear Leopard Seals?

A young fur seal stands at the edge of the rocks. Behind it, the colony: hundreds of warm, noisy bodies packed together. Safe. In front of it, the Southern Ocean. Grey. Calm. And completely silent.

It has been watching the water for a long time. Because somewhere under that surface is the one animal that every sea lion and fur seal in this part of the world has learned to fear.

It does not chase you across the open ocean. It waits right here, at the edge.

In one Antarctic colony, this hunter has taken around seven out of every ten pups born there, year after year. And the strangest part? Scientists think most of that killing comes down to just a handful of individuals.

This is the leopard seal. And this is why sea lions fear it.

# The eared seals

To understand the fear, you have to start with the hunted. Sea lions and fur seals belong to the same family: the eared seals. You can tell them apart from other seals by the tiny ears on the sides of their heads, and by the way they walk on land, propping themselves up on long front flippers.

On land, they are loud, social and surprisingly agile. In the water, they are fast, playful swimmers that chase fish and squid through the kelp. Most of the time, the ocean is where they are strongest. But far to the south, where the ice begins, that changes.

Antarctic fur seals breed on rocky beaches around the islands off the Antarctic Peninsula. Every summer, the mothers give birth on land, then leave their pups on shore for days at a time while they head out to sea to feed. For weeks, a pup's whole world is a strip of rock and the water's edge. And sooner or later, it has to learn to swim.

# Meet the leopard seal

Waiting for it is one of the most remarkable predators in the Southern Ocean.

The leopard seal is long, sleek and spotted, with a broad head that looks almost reptilian. Females are the bigger sex. They can grow to around three and a half metres long and weigh well over four hundred kilograms. Its jaws open astonishingly wide, and its front teeth are long, curved and built for gripping.

But look closer at the back of its mouth, and you find something you would never expect from a hunter like this. Its cheek teeth have three sharp points that lock together like a sieve. A leopard seal can take in a mouthful of seawater, push it out through those teeth, and keep the krill trapped inside.

Krill makes up a huge part of its diet. Fish, squid and penguins are on the menu too. But some leopard seals go after something much bigger. Other seals.

Leopard seals are the only seals that regularly hunt other warm-blooded animals. Crabeater seal pups. Fur seals. And, in parts of their range, young sea lions.

They are not only hunters. In the breeding season, the males sing. Long, haunting calls that drift for kilometres beneath the ice, and every male has his own song.

Most of the time, a leopard seal lives alone. It hauls out on drifting floes to rest between hunts, a solitary grey shape on the ice, and slips back into the water without a sound. On land it looks clumsy, almost lazy. In the water, it is a different animal: long front flippers, a flexible body, and sudden bursts of speed that most of its prey never see coming.

Almost nothing hunts the leopard seal in return. Its main natural enemy is the orca.

# The hunt

So how does a leopard seal catch a fur seal pup? Patience.

It patrols the shallows close to the colony, often near the ice edge or just off the beach, exactly where young seals have to enter the water. It stays low and quiet, sometimes hidden beneath floating ice, waiting for an inexperienced swimmer to leave the safety of the rocks.

Then it comes up from below. Fast. A pup that is still learning to swim has very little chance.

Leopard seals often shake their prey hard at the surface, tearing it into pieces small enough to swallow. Researchers watching from shore have seen it happen again and again during the pupping season.

Penguins know this problem well. At many Antarctic colonies, they gather at the edge of the ice and hesitate, sometimes for a long time, before plunging in together. A crowd is harder to single out than one bird on its own. Young fur seals do not have that option. When it is time to learn to swim, they go in where they were born, and the hunter knows exactly where that is.

For a young fur seal, the most dangerous moment of its life is not out in the open ocean. It is the first few metres from shore.

# The colony that collapsed

Nowhere has this been studied more closely than at Cape Shirreff, on Livingston Island, in the South Shetland Islands.

These fur seals have already survived one disaster. In the eighteen hundreds, sealers hunted them for their fur until they nearly disappeared. Over the following century, they made one of the great comebacks in the Southern Ocean. Scientists have counted the fur seals here for decades. And what they found recently is alarming.

Since two thousand and seven, this colony has shrunk by around eighty six percent. Every year since twenty ten, leopard seals have eaten an estimated seventy percent of the pups born here. In the worst seasons, around eight out of ten. Food shortages may play a part, but researchers believe the leopard seals are the main driver.

But here is the twist. It is not the whole leopard seal population doing this.

By reading chemical traces stored in their whiskers, researchers found that many of the leopard seals here are specialists: large adult females that focus on fur seal pups, season after season. One estimate suggests that as few as twenty of them may be behind most of the decline.

Twenty animals, reshaping the future of an entire colony.

# Living with fear

Fear like this does not only change how many seals survive. It changes how they live.

Ecologists have a name for it: the landscape of fear. When a predator like this is around, prey animals change where they go, when they move, and how long they stay in dangerous places. For an eared seal, the water's edge becomes somewhere to cross quickly, never somewhere to linger. The shoreline that should be a nursery becomes a gauntlet.

# Leopard seals and us

Humans have met leopard seals too. Divers and photographers often describe them as intensely curious. They follow boats, inspect cameras, and sometimes come uncomfortably close. Most encounters end peacefully. But not all.

In two thousand and three, a British marine biologist named Kirsty Brown was snorkelling near Rothera Research Station, off the Antarctic Peninsula, when a leopard seal pulled her under. She did not survive. It remains the only recorded human death caused by a leopard seal. Since then, researchers there watch the water for half an hour before anyone dives in.

It is a sobering reminder of what this animal is. Not a monster. A top predator, doing exactly what it evolved to do.

# Why the fear makes sense

So why do sea lions and fur seals fear the leopard seal?

Because it hunts them where they are weakest: at the edge, in the shallows, at the very moment the young first enter the sea. Because it is patient, powerful and, in some places, highly specialised. And because, for a young eared seal at the bottom of the world, the first swim can also be the last.

The story is not finished. As Antarctica warms and its sea ice changes, the balance between hunter and hunted is shifting too, and scientists are still working out what that will mean for both.

Somewhere in the Southern Ocean right now, a pup is looking at the water. And something beneath the ice is looking back.
